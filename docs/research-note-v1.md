# Distinguishing inferred regard from ability in language-model social judgments

**Working research note, v1.** Author: Mitchell Landers. Draft for review, not a claim that
peer review or the proposed mechanism study has been completed. Results below describe
four tested configurations; all conditions, including the negative one, are retained.

## Abstract

A language model can answer social questions consistently without maintaining a shared
representation of another person's regard. Before investigating that mechanism, a behavioral
instrument must distinguish regard-related predictions from ability judgments without creating
the difference through answer format. WTR-Bench's instrument-development sequence exposed
position sensitivity, censoring and explanation–choice mismatches in numerical formats, then
validated an ordinal comparative format on stipulated controls. A prospectively specified
social pilot applied that format to six held-out scenarios contrasting a person who tried
but could not help with a person who could help but refused. Claude Sonnet 4.5 favored the
former on future giving in 48/48 responses and the latter on same-task ability in 46/48.
A fixed Qwen3-14B non-thinking replication did not reproduce the dissociation. Two prospective
thinking extensions showed different patterns: Qwen3-14B recovered mainly the ability judgment,
whereas Qwen3-32B reproduced both net directions in all six scenarios, with 41/48 matched
crossovers. These findings establish a configuration-dependent behavioral dissociation in this
small scenario set. They do not identify a maintained valuation variable, estimate a scalar
welfare-tradeoff ratio, or measure the model's own social preferences. A local checkpoint bridge
and a calibrated causal-intervention study are specified separately.

## Why measurement came first

The motivating question is whether superficially coherent social behavior is generated by a
shared, relationally indexed internal state or by separate rules that happen to agree on familiar
inputs. A person who tries and fails may reveal willingness without demonstrating competence;
a capable refuser reveals competence without demonstrating willingness. That contrast supplies
an ordinal prediction that does not require the model to calculate a numerical latent weight.

The broader WTR-Bench project originally aimed to measure how a model's choices trade off its
own described payoff against another person's payoff. That Module A objective remains unmeasured.
The present study instead asks what a model predicts **about a described partner**. These are
different constructs. We use “regard-related” as a theoretical interpretation of future giving,
not as a direct observation of psychological regard or a fitted WTR parameter.

The empirical route was not a sequence of successful instruments. Early payoff ladders and
paired numerical formats exposed answer-position sensitivity, responses clustered near a bound,
and arithmetic or explanation–choice errors. The [216-request recovery diagnostic](recovery-36111049496-review.md)
separated explicit-weight recovery from history-derived recovery; the latter was mixed. The
[288-request payoff display diagnostic](presentation-36115733552-review.md) showed that clearer
tables could improve explanations without making choice-derived intervals trustworthy. These
failures constrain what later results can claim. They are not evidence that social inference
itself is absent.

The subsequent natural-language validation tested an ordinal format using stipulated evidence.
Version 3.2 produced 240/240 correct responses on its known-answer controls, with all 280
responses valid; 40 additional exploratory responses were not included as known-answer accuracy.
This was validation on the tested Sonnet configuration, not universal validation across models.
The social pilot then used held-out stories rather than stipulated target probabilities.

## Social pilot and fixed replications

Each story contrasts two acquaintances on separate occasions. One tries to help but cannot;
the other could help but refuses. The six tasks are moving a couch, fixing a faucet, making a
spreadsheet, translating, looking after a dog, and helping with a presentation. Three probes ask
about future giving, ability on the same task, and ability on a different task. The ability
probe stipulates that both people make a real effort and have enough time.

The 144-response schedule comprises six scenarios × three probes × two name assignments ×
two display positions × two repetitions: 72 distinct prompts. Repetitions and presentation
variants are checks within these six scenarios, not independent samples of social situations.
There is no social known-answer accuracy key. The fixed primary prediction is that the
tried-but-unable person is favored on giving and the able-but-unwilling person on same-task
ability. Equality and insufficient information are distinct options and remain separate from
invalid or missing responses.

Two evidence clarifications were added beyond the earlier pair renderer: the accounts describe
separate occasions, and relevant knowledge and physical limitations persist. Both were recorded
in the pilot plan; neither was part of the v3.2 controls. The different-task probe was exploratory.

The [Sonnet pilot](paired-pilot-v1.md) was followed by one
[fixed open-model replication](paired-open-v1.md), then
[two prospectively specified configuration extensions](paired-reasoning-v1.md) motivated by
that replication's negative result. All three Qwen conditions reused the exact 144-item stream.
The extensions each included one recorded arithmetic canary that checked reasoning/final-format
compatibility; arithmetic correctness did not select the model or gate collection. No social
answers were repaired from rationales or reasoning traces.

## Results

“Predicted crossover” means the same name/order/repetition form favors the unable person for
giving and the unwilling person for same-task ability. The scenario criterion requires both
predicted net directions in each scenario; it does not require every response to agree.

| Tested configuration | Giving: unable /48 | Same-task ability: unwilling /48 | Predicted crossovers /48 | Scenarios with both net directions /6 | Primary insufficient /96 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sonnet 4.5 | 48 | 46 | 46 | 6 | 2 |
| Qwen3-14B, non-thinking | 4 | 4 | 0 | 0 | 76 |
| Qwen3-14B, thinking | 5 | 43 | 4 | 4 | 48 |
| Qwen3-32B, thinking | 48 | 41 | 41 | 6 | 7 |

Sonnet's same-task exceptions were two insufficient-information responses, rather than choices
in the reverse direction. Its giving judgments were invariant across the tested name, order and
repeat pairs. The same-task probe had two name-pair and two order-pair disagreements, with no
repeat disagreement, arising from the dog scenario's abstentions.

The original Qwen3-14B configuration returned 144 valid, untruncated answers but 123/144 were
insufficient-information responses across all probes. It produced no predicted matched crossover.
This is a negative replication for the tested hosted FP8, reasoning-disabled, temperature-zero,
256-output-token, strict-JSON configuration. The 256-token cap was not reached in the observed
responses; a literal truncation explanation is therefore unsupported.

Thinking Qwen3-14B changed primarily on ability: 43/48 same-task judgments favored the capable
refuser, while 43/48 giving judgments still selected insufficient information. Its five directional
giving judgments all favored the person who tried; no primary directional response opposed the
prediction. This is selective recovery, not recovery of the full dissociation. Four scenarios
had both net signs, but there were only four matched crossovers overall.

Thinking Qwen3-32B favored the person who tried on giving in 48/48 and the capable refuser on
same-task ability in 41/48, with seven insufficient-information responses. It reproduced both
net directions in all six scenarios. Giving had zero name/order/repeat disagreements. Same-task
ability had 5/24 name, 7/24 order and 5/24 repeat disagreements. Sampling at temperature .6 means
these disagreements include stochastic variation and cannot isolate presentation bias.

This is not a uniform size gradient: 14B thinking produced more directional same-task ability
responses than 32B thinking (43 versus 41). The clearest difference is in giving. Nor does the
14B comparison isolate a causal effect of reasoning: reasoning, temperature, sampling controls
and token allowance changed together. Both thinking configurations used hosted FP8 weights,
temperature .6, top-p .95, top-k 20, min-p 0 and a 32,768-token combined allowance. Every one of
the 288 social responses was valid and untruncated. The two recorded canaries were also valid.

### What the explanations clarify—and do not establish

An unblinded assistant audit read all 288 thinking-extension brief bases and inspected 15
selected reasoning traces. This was post hoc qualitative interpretation, without independent
human coding or an inter-rater reliability estimate. The examples do not turn generated
explanations into faithful measurements of internal computation.

The 14B giving abstentions commonly treated the earlier helpful act as insufficient evidence
about a new points allocation. This supports an interpretation involving conservative transfer
or decision thresholds, while leaving capability/format explanations possible. In the 32B
same-task abstentions, brief bases treated the refuser's relevant knowledge as uncertain despite
the story's statement that the person could easily have helped. The model can therefore state
parts of the distinction while disagreeing about how much the wording licenses a prediction.

The different-task probe has a construct limitation. “Manage it” can be read as diligence or
reliability rather than skill, especially for tasks such as reviewing an application, watering
plants or giving a ride. Sonnet's different-task responses were 9 unable, 3 unwilling, 9 equal
and 27 insufficient. The corresponding counts were 0/0/3/45 for 14B thinking and 9/0/6/33 for
32B thinking. Some 32B rationales favored the willing person even across apparently distinct
skills, despite the effort stipulation. The earlier clean abstentions on certain Sonnet transfers
were therefore not uniformly replicated. This probe motivates redesign in a separately frozen
study; it should not be presented as successful domain-general ability separation.

## What this adds to the AI psychology argument

The positive results show that a single “always choose the nicer person” rule is insufficient
for the tested response pattern: the favored person changes with the primary question. The
negative and partial replications make the boundary of that behavioral finding visible. A model
may infer or express the ability distinction while declining the proposed giving transfer.

That does not identify how the distinction is implemented. Separate learned rules—roughly,
“tried to help predicts willingness” and “a continuing limitation predicts difficulty”—could
produce the observed dissociation without a shared valuation state. A shared relational state
could also produce abstentions if the decision policy demands stronger evidence. The behavioral
note therefore narrows the phenomenon to be explained without resolving the mechanism.

The contribution is a theory-derived, prospectively specified dissociation accompanied by an
instrument failure record, an explicit validation boundary, fixed replications and auditable raw
outputs. This is a credible small research note and a useful public work sample. The work does
not yet justify a general benchmark of model welfare-tradeoff ratios, broad claims about all
open models, or a discovery claim about internal social representations. No population-level
significance claim is made from these six selected scenarios.

## Next: bridge, then causal study

The [local checkpoint bridge](paired-local-v1.md) checks whether the model whose activations can
be inspected actually exhibits the behavior under a pinned local implementation. A positive
result is an entry condition for subsequent interventions, not an intervention result.

The [mechanism proposal](irv-mechanism-proposal-v0.2.md) asks whether an intervention on a
candidate representation of one person's regard for another selectively transfers across
judgments, after the diagnostic has been calibrated on constructed systems with known causal
structure. It distinguishes this relational target from ability, general positivity and answer
coding. It does not assume that a shared abstract variable must occupy one neuron or location.
The behavioral note can be published before that project is completed, with the proposal labeled
as future work. Claims about the origins of a learning architecture or compute advantages in the
broader EFLA program require separate experiments.
